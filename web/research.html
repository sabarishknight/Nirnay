How a home loan is approved in an Indian bank

Research note for a first meeting with the Retail Lending head of a mid-sized bank. October 2026.

In short: a home loan passes through seven stages and at least six people. Credit appraisal is mostly judgement on data that has to be gathered from many places; legal and technical verification is the longest stage and stays human. The biggest gains come from assembling the file early, checking documents against independent sources, and applying policy the same way every time.

Stages and roles

Checks and documents

  • Identity: PAN, Aadhaar or other officially valid document, photograph; CKYC record. Video-based KYC is permitted under the RBI KYC Master Direction.
  • Income, salaried: last three salary slips, Form 16 for two years, six months of bank statements. Officers cross-check net pay with salary credits.
  • Income, self-employed: two to three years of ITR with computation, audited financials, GST returns, twelve months of bank statements. Income is usually cash profit (after-tax income plus depreciation).
  • Repayment capacity: FOIR, the share of income going to EMIs including the new loan. Banks commonly cap it between 50% and 65% depending on income.
  • Credit history: bureau score (CIBIL is the reference most banks quote; 700 to 750 is a common floor), days past due, write-offs or settlements, recent enquiries.
  • Property: sale agreement or allotment letter, chain of title deeds, approved plan, occupancy or completion certificate, RERA registration for under-construction projects, society NOC for resale flats.
  • Security: valuation by an empanelled valuer, LTV within RBI caps, CERSAI search for existing charges, encumbrance certificate.

Rules that shape the process

  • LTV caps: 90% for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% from ₹30 lakh to ₹75 lakh, 75% above ₹75 lakh; stamp duty and registration excluded from property cost except for units up to ₹10 lakh. Risk weights are tied to LTV (35% up to 80% LTV, 50% above).
  • External benchmark pricing: since 1 October 2019 floating-rate retail loans from banks must be linked to an external benchmark such as the repo rate, with resets at least every three months. The repo rate rose from 5.25% to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, which raises EMIs on files already in progress.
  • Key Facts Statement: since 1 October 2024 every retail loan must be offered with a KFS showing the all-in Annual Percentage Rate, fees and repayment schedule, with time to accept.
  • CERSAI: security interests, including equitable mortgages, must be registered within 30 days of creation; lenders search the registry before sanction.
  • Release of documents: original property documents must be returned and CERSAI charges removed within 30 days of full repayment, or the lender pays ₹5,000 a day.
  • Pre-payment: no foreclosure or pre-payment charge on floating-rate loans to individuals for non-business purposes (directions of July 2025, for loans sanctioned from 1 January 2026).
  • Fair practices and data: rejection reasons are communicated in writing; consent is required for bureau and Account Aggregator data, and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 governs personal data.

Turnaround

Lenders quote 7 to 15 working days to sanction and up to about 30 days to final disbursement, faster for salaried applicants (about 4 to 5 days in the best case) and slower for self-employed applicants (7 to 10 days) and resale properties, where title checks go deeper. Legal and technical verification is consistently described as the most time-consuming stage. The synthetic book in this demo averages about 18 working days from login to sanction, which reflects a mid-sized bank with manual appraisal rather than a digital-first lender.

Pain points

  1. Chasing and re-keying. Officers gather data from the LOS, core banking, portals and PDFs and re-type it into the appraisal note.
  2. The long pole is legal and technical. It depends on external advocates and valuers and starts late because the file is incomplete.
  3. Inconsistency. FOIR, income and eligibility are computed in personal spreadsheets; two branches can reach different answers on the same facts.
  4. Fraud. Inflated salary slips, forged or duplicate title documents and multiple financing on the same property are recurring patterns flagged by RBI and NHB.
  5. Policy drift. A rate or policy change applies to new files, while files already in progress keep their old assessment.
  6. Customer opacity. Applicants rarely know which stage their file is in or what is missing.

What it means for the design

  • Assemble the whole file in one place at login (lakehouse) so legal and technical can start on day one.
  • Use AI to read documents and draft, not to decide or calculate. Rules and arithmetic run in code and cite the clause.
  • Check documents against independent sources (bank credits, Form 16, EPFO, GST, CERSAI), because fraud lives in the gap between them.
  • Keep every decision with the authority in the delegation of powers; make overrides easy but recorded.
  • Treat policy as data, so a change can be applied to files already in progress.

Practitioner input

How these findings were checked. Every rule on this page was checked against the RBI circular itself, not a summary; turnaround ranges come from lender disclosures. No interviews with banking practitioners were held before submission. Before the walkthrough, three claims will be tested with credit staff: legal and technical is the longest stage; FOIR deviations are the most common reason a file goes to a higher authority; and officers would accept a drafted note for sanction if every line shows its source.
